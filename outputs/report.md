Noise: each run draws actual R ~ R*(1+N(0,1 %)), C ~ C*(1+N(0,2 %)), and adds N(0, 10 ps) measurement noise. Seeded with `numpy.random.default_rng(2026)`.

R^2 (log scale) = 0.99899.

Note that interactions depend on the scale of the response: on the log scale R x C vanishes (log of a product is a sum) while R x V becomes large (log(R + R_on(V)) is not additive in R and V). 'Is there an interaction?' always means 'on which scale?'.

Lack-of-fit test: F = 5.02 on (5, 11) df, p = 0.012. **The quadratic shape is detectably wrong somewhere in the box**, so prediction intervals will understate the real error (see validation).

Within the tested bounds the optimiser pushes R and C to their lower limits and raises V until the energy budget binds. In a real design C is usually fixed by the load being driven, so the `C = 3 pF` panel of `response_surface.png` is the more realistic view.

r2                                   0.97                       0.9727                        0.9353
```
95 % prediction-interval coverage: 60% (nominal 95 %).

The log-scale model is clearly better than the same quadratic fitted to delay in ps. Coverage below nominal is the practical symptom of the lack of fit found above: the intervals account for noise, not for the quadratic's systematic error. The right panel of `validation.png` shows the pattern: errors trend from positive at low V to negative at high V, because R_on ~ (V - 0.5)^-1.3 is not quadratic even on the log scale.

xR:xV (1600 runs)     -13.48          -10.46 2.129e-11                       True                    False
```
The true R x V effect built into the simulator is -10.46 ps (from the C_J(V) parasitic). In the 16-run study it is not statistically detectable. Repeating the same factorial 200 times (1600 runs, cheap in simulation, absurd on a bench) makes it highly significant, yet it is still far below anything an engineer would act on.
